How a samvaad can heal

6 minute read

Samvaad means dialogue: not two people taking turns to speak, but two people actually meeting in the middle. When that happens, even old hurt begins to loosen.

Pain wants to be witnessed

Much of the ache after a fight is not the words themselves. It is the feeling of not being understood, of carrying something heavy that the other person cannot see. That is why an apology that skips the listening often lands flat, while "I can see why that hurt you" can soften a whole evening.

Being heard does not require the other person to agree. It requires them to be curious about what it was like to be you in that moment.

Naming a feeling quiets it

Something simple happens when we put an emotion into words. In a well-known study at UCLA, people who named the feeling in an image ("angry", "scared") showed less activity in the brain's alarm centre than people who simply looked at it. Researchers call this affect labelling.

In a relationship, this is the difference between "you are impossible" and "I am feeling alone right now". The second is quieter, truer, and far easier for the other person to hear.

Looking at it from a little distance

When we replay a painful moment from inside it, we tend to relive it: the same heat, the same words. Psychologist Ethan Kross and colleagues found that stepping back, seeing the moment as if from a few feet away, helps people make sense of it without being swept up again.

This is part of why Samvaad plays your conversation back before saying anything about it. Watching it as an observer, rather than as the person who was hurt, makes room for a second look.

Before you begin: one minute of breath

Hard conversations go better from a settled body. This is the same rhythm Samvaad offers when a conversation runs hot.

  1. Breathe in through the nose for a slow count of four.
  2. Hold gently for four.
  3. Breathe out through the mouth for six, letting the shoulders drop.
  4. Soften for two: jaw, hands, the space between the eyebrows.

Four rounds is about a minute. A longer out-breath than in-breath is a simple, well-loved way to tell the body it is safe to slow down.

Five steps back to each other

1. Pause

If either of you is flooded, stop and agree on a time to return. Healing does not happen in the middle of the storm.

2. Name what you feel

One sentence, starting with "I". "Mujhe laga ki meri baat kisi ko matter nahi karti."

3. Listen to understand

When it is their turn, your only job is to understand. Try saying back what you heard: "So when I didn't call, you felt forgotten. Is that right?"

4. Repair

Own your part, however small. "Mera tone galat tha. I'm sorry." Repair is not weakness; it is what keeps two people close through years of ordinary friction.

5. Come back to it

Choose one small thing to do differently, and check in a few days later. Healing is rarely one big conversation. It is many small ones that go a little better each time.

The goal is not a conversation without hurt. It is a relationship where hurt can be talked about.
